Pixel World — app map

A take-home guide to the code you built: what the app is, how a thing you see on screen is actually made, and where to change it.

What this app is

A 2D side-view pixel sandbox. Every single cell in the grid is a material, and each material has its own rules: sand falls and piles, water flows and pools, fire spreads on its own, lava crusts water into stone. You walk around, and you carry three tools — a spark wand, dynamite and an acid flask — that let you tear the world apart. There is no score and no goal; the point is to mess with it.

It is three pieces, and they never talk to each other directly:

PieceWhat it doesWhere it lives
The world A grid of cells, endless sideways. It is cut into 256-cell-wide strips ("chunks"); each strip is built once, from its own number, and kept for good, so anything you break stays broken. src/world.h, src/world.cpp
The recipe Builds a strip of terrain from its number: land, dirt, rock, caves, water, lava, snow, and the hand-made ruins and trees that stand on it. src/generation.cpp
The rules Each frame, every cell near you follows the rules of its material — falling, flowing, burning, reacting with its neighbours. Cells off-screen are left alone and wait. src/sim.cpp, src/materials.cpp

One action, end to end

You hold D and walk to the right. The land rises into a snow-capped mountain. Here is every place that had to happen for that to be true.

A reference route through the code

Three stops, each about ten minutes. This is a map for finding your way back in, not a record of a tour — use it in any order you like.

Stop 1 The world: a grid, and the strips it comes in

src/world.h → src/world.cpp

Start at World::at, World::set and World::mass in src/world.h:88. Those three are the whole vocabulary of the simulation: every rule in the app is a question asked about one cell, or a change written to one cell.

Then World::chunk (src/world.h:120): the store that keeps built strips around forever. Note the two jobs it does — it is both the place a new strip is written into and the reason your damage survives.

Try this: open src/world.cpp and find where at() decides what happens if you ask for a cell outside the world. That one line is the "the player cannot fall out of the bottom of the world" promise in the PRD.

Stop 2 The recipe: how a strip of land is built

src/generation.cpp

generateChunk (src/generation.cpp:486) is the whole recipe in one function, and it reads top to bottom like the layers of the world: sky, then land and ground, then caves, then the buried pockets of sand, gravel and clay, then the pond and the lava cup, then the trees and ruins standing on top.

The land itself comes from three noises stacked on each other — landAt (src/generation.cpp:441): a great swell that raises the whole world over thousands of columns, broad hills on top of that, and mountain ranges from folded ("ridged") noise. The snow is not a line across the map; snowRowsAt (src/generation.cpp:482) asks how high that column's mountain is and gives it a cap of snow to match.

isCave (src/generation.cpp:157) is the other half: it reads the cave noise in depth below the surface, so a cavern sits the same distance under the ground everywhere, and it refuses to hollow out anything shallower than kCaveRoof (src/generation.cpp:18).

Try this: change kCaveThreshold (src/generation.cpp:22) from 0.67 to 0.55, rebuild, and look at how much more of the rock is hollow. Then put it back.

Stop 3 The rules: what each material does

src/materials.cpp → src/sim.cpp

src/materials.cpp:16 is a table with one row per material: density, whether it falls, whether it flows, friction, what it melts into, whether it burns. Everything the simulation knows about sand is on sand's row. Change a number there and every sand in the world changes with it.

src/sim.cpp is the engine. Simulation::step (src/sim.cpp:642) walks the visible region once a frame; flowMass (src/sim.cpp:383) is the rule that moves liquid and loose grains a cell at a time; reactPair (src/sim.cpp:186) is the rule for two touching materials (lava + water → stone, fire + snow → water); updateFire (src/sim.cpp:79) is the rule that fire spreads on its own.

Try this: the table has a friction number for loose solids. Set sand's to 200 and a sand pile stands up steeper than it did. It is a one-number edit, and you can undo it with git checkout src/materials.cpp.

Bonus stop: your tools, the player and the HUD

src/tools.cpp holds the three tools: Tools::fire (src/tools.cpp:66) throws whatever you picked toward the mouse, Tools::tick (src/tools.cpp:104) moves it, and Tools::detonate (src/tools.cpp:182) sets it off. A detonation goes to blast in src/blast.cpp:62, which is the one place that decides how much rock an explosion removes.

src/player.cpp is you: Player::tick (src/player.cpp:69) walks, jumps and falls, and Player::pushLoose (src/player.cpp:156) is the rule that lets you shove a light heap of sand along but not a heavy one. src/hud.cpp draws the health bar and the name of the tool you are holding.

To change this, start here

You want to…Start at
Make the land bigger, flatter or more mountainous landAt and mountainHeightAt in src/generation.cpp
Move the snow, or make it deeper snowRowsAt in src/generation.cpp
Make the caves deeper, rarer or bigger kCaveRoof and kCaveThreshold at the top of src/generation.cpp
Change how one material behaves Its row in the table in src/materials.cpp
Change what happens when two materials touch reactPair in src/sim.cpp
Make an explosion bigger or weaker blast in src/blast.cpp
Add a new material The Mat enum and the table in src/materials.h and src/materials.cpp — the enum order and the table order must match, and there is a static_assert that checks it

A practice to reuse

Measure the thing you care about, where it lives. During the bug round, a check called pond keeps its water kept failing — but the pond was fine. The check was counting the water in the whole world, and a deep lava cup had melted some clay nearby into water. Correct behaviour, wrong measurement. The fix was to count the water in the pond's own columns.

The same round found every other bug the same way: by measuring the generated world rather than by reading the code and guessing — how much water was in a pond, how many columns had sunk, how many snow grains had moved, how big the biggest cavern was. Each of those became a self-test that fails if the bug ever comes back.

That is the reusable part: when something looks wrong, write down the number you would expect if it were right, then measure it. A number that disagrees with you is worth more than another careful read of the code.

Running it again

brew install raylib pkg-config   # once
make run                          # compile and play
./pixelworld --selftest           # 46 checks, all passing
./pixelworld --smoke              # renders frames headlessly, writes smoke.png

Controls: A/D or ←/→ to walk, Space to jump, 1/2/3 to pick the spark wand, dynamite or acid flask, left mouse to use it, R to throw the world away and start again.
