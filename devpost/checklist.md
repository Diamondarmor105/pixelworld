  Why now: Proves the riskiest unknown first — that raylib builds and that a grid of material numbers can be turned into chunky pixels on screen. Everything else lands on this path, so it is bootstrapping and first behavior in one slice.
  PRD ref: `prd.md > Screens and Layout`, `prd.md > Look and Feel`
  Spec ref: `spec.md > Stack`, `spec.md > Components > Grid and world storage`, `spec.md > Components > Rendering`, `spec.md > File Structure`, `spec.md > Where It Runs and How Someone Tries It`
  Build: Write the `Makefile` (clang++, `-std=c++17`, pkg-config for raylib) with `make`, `make run`, `make debug`. Create `src/materials.h` (material IDs + starter color table), `src/world.h/.cpp` (flat `std::vector<uint8_t>` grid, 480×270, bounds-checked `materialAt`/`setMaterial`), `src/render.h/.cpp` (visible grid → raylib image buffer → nearest-neighbour `Texture2D` drawn at 3×), and `src/main.cpp` (window + main loop, Esc quits). Add a `--selftest` mode that renders 60 frames, writes `selftest.png`, prints a one-line summary, and exits — this is the mechanical check reused by every later slice. Add a hand-made static scene (sky, packed dirt and stone ground, a sand column) so there is something to look at. Write `README.md` (build, run, controls) and extend `.gitignore` for the compiled binary and `selftest.png`.
  Verify (mechanical): `make` exits 0 with no errors; `./pixelworld --selftest` prints `grid 480x270 | wrote selftest.png` plus two PASS lines and exits 0; `sips -g pixelWidth -g pixelHeight selftest.png` reports 480×270; `./pixelworld --smoke` opens the window, renders 60 frames, reports the world covering at least 99% of the frame, and exits 0.
  Learner check: Run `make run`. You should see a bright sky, brown ground, and a visible pile of sand grains, each one a chunky block — not blurry. Press Esc to close. Does it look like a pixel world yet?

  Why now: This is the first real piece of the kernel. Everything else in the project is a different rule applied to this same per-pixel step, so it must exist and be trustworthy before anything is layered on it.
  PRD ref: `prd.md > Materials and how they behave`, `prd.md > Acceptance criteria`
  Spec ref: `spec.md > Components > Materials table`, `spec.md > Components > Simulation`, `spec.md > Data Model`
  Build: Fill `src/materials.h` with all 24 learner-specified materials as a single static table (id, name, category, color, weight, flammable, melts-into, dissolves-in, explosion resistance) — this is the table that makes adding materials cheap. Simulation covers the visible region plus a 16-pixel margin. Keep the `--selftest` scenes as the sand-and-water check.
  Verify (mechanical): `./pixelworld --selftest` prints and passes `sand settles: PASS` (after 2000 steps no sand cell has air directly beneath it, and the pile's top row is well below where the column started) and `water spreads and stays put: PASS` (water poured in one place keeps every cell, covers at least a quarter more columns than it started with, and none of it leaves the basin or climbs above the rim).

## Revisions

- Slice 5's ninth bug pass (learner: the water "does not level out and remains stuck in a jagged staircase", with a written analysis blaming the surface guard and the equal-density water-on-water rule):
  - **The symptom is real and reproducible.** Poured into a stepped basin, water settles as a clean **45-degree slope** under the simple rules, and it is **byte-identical at tick 300 and tick 3000** - frozen, not merely slow. Measured on that scene: 200 water cells, and of them **150 were held by the surface guard** and every one of those 150 sat on the slope, with **0 cells having any downward escape**. So the guard is not "holding a flat surface still" there; it is pinning a slope.
  - **Both proposed fixes were built and measured, and both fail.** (A) Treating a neighbouring flowing liquid as passable disables the guard entirely, because in a pool the neighbours *are* liquid: the surface metrics fall straight back to the pre-fix numbers (**55 of 480 columns, 11000 profile changes**) - it simply trades the staircase for the shimmer of the eighth pass - and the staircase shape is **unchanged**. (B) The equal-density observation is correct as code (water cannot swap with water) but is not a cause: the sideways step needs to move into *air*, not to swap with water, and the staircase has no air within reach.
  - **A third fix was authored here and also rejected.** A local "creep toward the nearest strictly lower column" rule was built and measured: it made things **worse** - `a settled pool does not pulse` went from 8 of 480 columns to **324 of 480** (profile changes 53048), and `a big lake spreads and settles` went from `moving 0` to `moving 204` - while the staircase itself was **unchanged**, because a cell can only move one step and the column it lands in is no longer lower on the next tick. Reverted.
  - **Root cause, stated plainly.** This is not a bug in the guard. It is the direct consequence of the simple-rules decision from the fifth pass: a liquid may fall, roll diagonally and step sideways, and **nothing in those three moves lets a cell high on a slope travel to a lower column**. A 45-degree slope is genuinely stable under them. The same trade-off was already measured and put to the learner in the fifth pass (a pool cannot be both flat and perfectly still without restoring an "is the neighbour lower?" comparison), and they chose simple rules and accepted the mound. The staircase is that decision appearing in a harsher shape than the test scene had shown.
  - **The learner was shown the fork again, with the options measured, and chose to keep the simple rules and accept the staircase.** No code change. Recorded here because the alternative - a periodic levelling pass that rewrites a body of water to its exact-volume surface row - remains the only way to get genuinely flat water, and that is the decision to revisit if flat water is ever wanted.
  - **One apparent defect investigated and found not to be one.** The scene shows a detached 3-cell patch below the step, and a connected-body scan confirms **2 bodies** (197 + 3). Inspecting the geometry, those 3 cells sit on the *lower* floor at the foot of a 10-row cliff - exactly where water that spilled over the edge would land - and they are on the floor, so the guard correctly leaves them free to creep until the stone wall and slope box them in. Nothing is stranded through a wall; it is the correct result.
  - Working tree returned to the committed state (`f676795`); `--selftest` stays at 21 checks, all passing.

- Slice 5's eighth bug pass (learner supplied a written analysis of the remaining surface shimmer, proposing that the per-cell `lean` preference flips as a particle crosses a cell boundary, that the two sweeps cancel each other, and that flat surfaces need a "resting" check):
  - **One of the three claims held up, one was disproved, and one was exactly right in spirit but wrong in detail.**
  - **Claim 1 - the `lean` preference is position-based and flips under a moving particle - is real.** Measured directly: **50.4%** of horizontally adjacent cells have opposing `lean`, so half of all sideways moves land a particle on a cell that wants to push it the other way. On a settled pool, 40 of the 68 moves in one tick crossed an opposing-`lean` boundary. The observation was correct; it turned out not to be the dominant cause, because a settled pool's *middle* was already measured at 0 moves.
  - **Claim 2 - the second sweep's `-lean` fights the first sweep - is not correct, and this was disproved by experiment.** A cell that moved in sweep 1 is marked, and the sideways-only sweep returns immediately on a marked cell (line 301's `isMoved` check), so it is never asked to move twice in one tick. Changing sweep 2 from `-lean` to `+lean`, the proposed Solution A, produced a **byte-identical** surface profile (11000 vs 11000 changes; middle-of-pool 0 vs 0). A null change, so it was not applied.
  - **Claim 3 - flat surfaces shuffle for no gain - was right, and the proposed test was the wrong one but pointed straight at the cause.** Solution B as suggested (skip a liquid sandwiched between the same liquid on both sides) was also **byte-identical** (11000 vs 11000), because the cells that shimmer are all at *boundaries* - the top film, the contact rows, the wall - and none of them are sandwiched. Instrumenting the movers instead gave the real diagnosis: of the 34 cells that still moved on a settled pool, **25 could neither fall nor roll** and were taking a pure sideways shuffle every tick. That is the mechanism the learner described in point 3.
  - **What fixed it.** A liquid that has **nowhere to fall and nowhere to roll** - so it is part of a settled pool's surface - takes no sideways step at all. The test is deliberately narrow: the cell must also be sitting **on liquid** (part of a pool's surface, not on the floor), so a cell resting on the ground keeps creeping outward to reach lower ground. Dropping the `onLiquid` condition was tried and **rejected**: it held a pool back from spreading at all (41 columns of spread fell to 24) and the `water spreads to a shallow heap` check failed. The openness tests are ordered cheapest-first so the guard rejects most cells on a single comparison.
  - **Result, measured on a wide shallow pool.** Surface profile changes over 200 ticks fell from **11000 to 1600**, and the affected columns from **55 of 480 to 8 of 480**. The whole of what remains is confined to x=0 (the world edge) and x=446..452 (the pool's right fringe); the other **472 columns never move at all**. Spreading improved at the same time: `water spreads to a shallow heap` went from 41 columns at mean depth 1 to **54 columns at mean depth 1** - wider and flatter.
  - **A performance scare that was measurement noise, not a regression.** Single-run timings suggested the guard cost 2 ms/tick, and one run read 16.80 ms; re-measured with 15 repeats of 50 ticks and taking the minimum and median, the guard is free: base min 6.10 / median 6.91, final min **6.02** / median **6.91**. The expensive-looking version was the un-reordered guard, which duplicated the `shoveGrain` calls; dropping that duplication and ordering the openness tests cheapest-first recovered everything.
  - The self-test `a settled pool does not pulse` now measures the **surface profile** (the topmost water row in each column) rather than only the middle, because the middle was already still and that was what hid the shimmer. It asserts how many columns move, not the raw change count, so one column shifting every tick reads the same as one shifting once. Confirmed not vacuous against both bug variants: with the guard removed it **FAILS (55 of 480)** and with the original alternating scan restored it **FAILS (73 of 480)**, against **8 of 480** fixed. `--selftest` is 21 checks, all passing; smoke test unchanged at 99.9% world coverage.

- Slice 5's seventh bug pass (learner: the water surface "looks semi-transparent or flickery", diagnosed as temporal ping-ponging from the per-frame flip of `dir = (frame_ % 2 == 0) ? 1 : -1`):
  - **The learner's diagnosis was correct, and the code was exactly where they said.** The falling sweep walked left-to-right on even ticks and right-to-left on odd ones. Because a liquid is allowed a down-diagonal step, the scan order decides whether a pool edge drains into the gap below-left or fills it - so the edge reached one settled shape on even ticks and a **different** settled shape on odd ones. Dumped from inside `step`, the surface of a wide shallow pool alternated between `....W..WWWW` and `.....WWWWWW` on consecutive ticks, forever. Both shapes are genuinely settled for the scan that produced them; they simply disagree. The eye blends the alternation into a flickering, semi-transparent surface.
  - **Removing the flip outright is worse, and this was measured rather than assumed.** With a fixed global direction the whole pool leans one way and churns harder: oscillating surface cells rose from **90 to 431** and moves per tick from **90 to 313**. So the flip was load-bearing and could not simply be deleted.
  - **Two attempts that did not work, both measured and reverted.** (1) A per-*frame* per-cell hash for the direction: oscillation fell 90 to 60, but cells that wandered off and came back **tripled, to 276** - a preference that changes every tick just flip-flops out of phase with its neighbour. (2) A local "levelling pass" that pinned lone cells: **no effect at all** (58 stayed 58), because the cells involved have a legitimate fall available and are not actually lone. Both were dropped rather than left in.
  - **What fixed it.** The scan order is now **fixed** (bottom-up left-to-right for the falling sweep, top-down right-to-left for the sideways sweep), and the left/right *bias* is balanced the other way: every cell takes a **standing** preference from `noise(x, y, 0)` - its own coordinates only, so it never changes from tick to tick and neighbouring cells lean different ways. `updateCell` still tries the other side when the first is blocked, so nothing is unreachable.
  - **Result, measured on a wide shallow pool.** The middle of the pool (x 60..340, rows 188..195) moves **0 cells over 200 ticks** where it used to move **5788**. The deep interior is completely still; `a big lake spreads and settles` improved from `moving 186` to `moving 0`. What still moves is confined to thin boundaries: the two contact rows, the ragged top film, and the wall the water spreads against - about 2 cells per row, concentrated in the first and last few columns, which is the known "thin layers keep single-cell gaps" limitation rather than the lockstep pulse.
  - **Honest remaining limitation:** the pool is *not* perfectly static. The free surface profile still shifts on most ticks because of those boundary cells, so at a pool's extreme edges a slight shimmer remains. It is a different, much smaller effect than the pulse that was reported, and it is not yet fixed.
  - New self-test `a settled pool does not pulse`, watching the middle of the pool for 200 ticks. Confirmed not vacuous: with the alternating scan restored it **FAILS (5788)** and with the fix it PASSES (**0**). `--selftest` is now 21 checks, all passing. Tick cost 6.28 ms/tick (was 6.09) - about 2% for the per-cell hash, well inside budget.

- Slice 5's sixth bug pass (learner: "moving the player around causes the movement of other falling solids, and those falling solids blocks at the surface jitter in place"):
  - **The second (sideways) sweep let falling solids step sideways.** The sideways step is meant for liquids only, and the first sweep enforces that by returning early for a solid. But the sideways-only second sweep skips that early return, so a falling solid fell through to the sideways step and took one horizontal cell on **every tick** - a grain on a heap's surface that jittered left and right forever. It only showed up while walking because that is when the second sweep had rows to visit. Fixed with a guard that lives outside the first-sweep block: a cell whose material does not flow returns before the sideways step, in both sweeps. Found by instrumenting `trySwap` and logging `HORIZONTAL falling-solid swap` - which is a move a falling solid should never be able to make.
  - **A moving camera made distant grain twitch.** Measured with a window panned across a fully-settled scene: 616 cells moved on a single extra full-grid step, and the same grains moved again on a second pass - the whole world was in a permanent low-level churn, which the panning region merely exposed. Every one of those moves was a falling solid taking a horizontal step, so the same sideways-step guard fixed both symptoms at once. The loose-solid churn is now **gone**: a fully-settled scene moves **0** loose solids over 300 ticks of full-grid stepping, **0** with the region parked, and **0** with the region panning across it. (A separate idea - clipping `trySwap` so a grain could never reach a pixel outside the region, treating the edge as a wall - was tried and **rejected**: it made the churn worse, 8/20/2 loose moves per sweep became 11/85/58, because it piles grain at the edge that then collapses when the edge moves on.)
  - What is left moving while you walk is 100% within 24 cells of the player (the ploughing you asked for from slice 5), and **0** cells further away.
  - Verified end to end with the real game loop (player tick, camera follow/clamp, region-limited step): walking the player back and forth for 1500 ticks now moves loose solids **only** within 24 cells of the player.
  - New self-test `a settled scene stays still under a panning region`, which settles a scene with three loose piles then pans a 480-wide window across it, asserting that **no** loose solid moves. Confirmed it is not a vacuous test: with the sideways-step bug restored it **FAILS (327 loose solids moved)** and with the fix it PASSES (0). `--selftest` now has 20 checks, all passing. Tick cost unchanged at ~6.1 ms/tick on the playable scene.

- Slice 5's fifth bug pass (learner: "The speed of the lake settling is not an issue. Try to make the water and the falling pixels follow simple rules."):
  - **The liquid rules were cut down to the plainest possible set.** The old liquid code had grown five interacting mechanisms - a pressure scan, a sideways reach for the nearest empty cell, a grain-shove, a depth measurement (`liquidBelow`) and the periodic bulk-levelling pass (`levelLiquid`) - and the learner asked for rules simple enough to explain in one breath. The whole pile is gone. Falling solids now do exactly two things (down, then down-diagonal); liquids do exactly three (down, then down-diagonal, then sideways). The only extra kept is `shoveGrain`, which the learner explicitly asked to keep, so a liquid that cannot fall or step aside still pushes a lighter grain out of the way and melt-water still escapes a snow bank.
  - **The periodic levelling pass was removed entirely**, as the learner chose. Water is now genuinely a slippery granular material rather than a true fluid: it runs downhill and spreads out, but it does **not** find a dead-flat level.
  - **A tick now runs the rules in two sweeps** - bottom-up (which does the falling, so a cell lands before the cell above it moves) and then top-down, sideways-only (which fills the gaps the first sweep leaves behind). Both share the same "moved" marks, so no cell moves twice in a tick. The sideways-only second sweep is what lets a raised pile spread instead of freezing as a staircase. To keep the second sweep nearly free, it only revisits the rows the first sweep actually moved something in, so an empty sky or a settled pool pays nothing extra: the playable scene ticks in **6.4 ms** (was 9.6 ms with a full second sweep) and an empty grid in 5.7 ms, both back to the old baseline.
  - **A settle-and-shimmer trade-off was measured and put to the learner.** With purely local rules a pool cannot be both flat and perfectly still: flat needs a "is the neighbour lower?" comparison back, which is the rule the learner asked to drop. A big lake was measured settling as a broad mound (middle **18 rows higher** than the edges) with about **8%** of water pixels shuffling each tick. The learner chose to **keep the purely simple rules and accept the mound**, so that is what ships: water looks and behaves like a very slippery grain, consistent with sand and gravel.
  - Two self-test checks that encoded the removed machinery were rewritten to match the agreed behaviour. `water spreads to a flat surface` became `water spreads to a shallow heap` (asserts the pour spreads across the floor and no column towers over the average depth, instead of requiring zero stray tops). `a big lake levels and settles` became `a big lake spreads and settles` (asserts the water reaches both ends of the floor, volume is conserved, the surface has no spikes, and less than a tenth of the cells are still moving - instead of requiring a one-row-flat, dead-still surface). `--selftest` still has 19 checks, all passing.
  - The per-cell `flowSlack` field is no longer read by movement at all; it is left in the material table (with a comment saying so) purely so the table and the spec still line up, rather than editing 24 rows for no behavioural gain.

- Liquid system review (learner: "review and improve upon the current systems, especially the water flowing system"):
  - **A large body of water cost more than a whole frame.** A full-width settled lake measured 24.5 ms/tick on the 1440×313 grid, against a 16.7 ms budget for 60 fps. The cost was not the engine walking the grid - an empty scene and a 50-row layer of stone both tick in ~5.9 ms - and it was not the periodic leveling pass either (settled *lava*, which skips leveling, cost the same 24.9 ms). It was the surface-depth comparison: it ran for **every liquid cell**, including the thousands of buried interior cells of a deep pool, which can never move for that reason, and each one counted the liquid all the way down to the bottom of the world. On a whole-screen lake that was tens of millions of reads a tick, all returning "nothing to do".
  - Two changes, both behaviour-preserving. (1) **Levelling is now a surface-only job**: a cell with liquid stacked directly on top of it (pressure > 0) returns before the comparison, because its only business is moving under pressure, and the periodic pass levels a pool's interior in one step anyway. (2) **The depth comparison is bounded**: the cell's own depth is counted once, and the neighbour scan stops after `myDepth + 1` rows, which is already enough to prove whether the neighbour is deeper. Result: the full-width lake dropped from 24.5 to **10.6 ms/tick** (2.3× faster) - comfortably inside the frame budget - and it will matter in slice 6, where the generated world will be full of big water bodies.
  - Verified the physics did not change: deep column spreading (70 columns), a column of 250 cells spreading across 106 columns, oil rising to float on water (all cells conserved, no oil left below the water), draining through a hole, a 1-wide channel, and water bursting onto a sand pile all give the same result as before; a settled lake is still **0 cells/tick**.
  - `--selftest` now has 19 checks, adding `a big lake levels and settles`: a wide, deep, deliberately uneven body of water must end up flat (within a row), completely still, and with every cell conserved.

- Slice 5's fourth bug pass (learner: "the water still has a few pixels that are in constant jittering state on the surface"):
  - **A single water pixel blinked forever on a pool's surface.** The surface-hop rule chose where to step by comparing the **top row** of neighbouring columns. That looks right but is self-defeating: the top row of the column the cell just left drops by one the moment it leaves, so the cell always sees somewhere "lower" and hops back, then sees the original column as lower again - one cell trading spots with an air cell every single tick. Measured on a pool on a stepped floor: `(19,57)` and `(20,57)` changing 60/60 ticks.
  - The rule now compares the **depth of liquid below the cell** instead (`liquidBelow`), which does not move when the cell leaves, so an already-level pool is a genuine tie and the cell stays put. The old `columnTop` helper is gone.
  - Result: water-related movement in the full playable scene is now **0 cells/tick** (the 1 cell/tick that remains is lava/sulfur crusting in the crater, not water); a pool on a stepped floor went from 2 cells/tick to 0; and a shallow pool spread on a flat floor now settles with **0 stray top cells** instead of 27 (the ragged surface that shimmered). Leveling also got **faster** - a tall column into a wide tank reads spread 5 rows at tick 60 and 1 by tick 600 (was 9 and 1 by 900).
  - `--selftest` now has 18 checks: the new `water spreads to a flat surface` counts columns whose water sits a row above both neighbours, which is exactly the blinking-cell shape. Verified it is not a vacuous test - with the old comparison restored it **FAILS (27 stray cells)** and with the fix it PASSES (0). Performance unchanged at 6.3 ms/tick on the full 1440x313 grid.

- Slice 5's third bug pass (learner: water "doesn't level out properly", and the loose-block push should depend on weight):
  - **Water levelled only approximately, and painfully slowly.** The local rules alone left a poured pool a few rows uneven and, worse, a tall column collapsing into a wide tank took *thousands* of ticks to even out (measured: spread 34 rows at tick 60, still 4 rows at tick 12000, never fully flat). The surface hop now reaches out to `flowSlack` columns for the nearest strictly-lower column instead of only looking one cell away, which helps but is not enough on its own — the real fix is a new periodic **leveling pass** (`levelLiquid`, every `LEVEL_INTERVAL` = 20 ticks). It flood-fills each connected body of a flowing liquid (8-connected, so a diagonal staircase is one body), reads each column's support row, solves for the single surface row that holds the body's exact volume, and rewrites the body to it. It conserves the cell count exactly and is a **no-op on an already-level pool**, so it never reintroduces shimmer. Measured on the same tank: spread 9 rows at tick 60, 2 at tick 180, 1 by tick 600; the whole playable scene now moves **fewer** cells per tick than before (16 vs 45) because the pass leaves settled water alone. Lava is skipped (it is thick and crusts rather than flowing). Cost on the full 1440×313 grid: 6.2 ms/tick, well inside the 16.7 ms a 60 fps frame allows.
  - **The push distance is now based on the weight of the grain, not a fixed 3 columns.** `pushLoose` adds up the density of every cell it would have to move and gives up once the total passes `PUSH_FORCE` (1200), so the reach falls out of the material's own density rather than being a single number for everything. A 4-cell-tall heap is ploughed through 4 columns deep if it is snow (density 90), 3 if it is soil (145), 2 if sand (160), and gravel (170) stops you first; heavier/taller heaps stop you sooner. Previously snow and gravel were treated identically.
  - `--selftest` now has 17 checks: `player pushes sand` became `player pushes a light heap`, and a new `a heavy heap stops the player` locks in the weight dependence.

- Slice 5's second bug pass (three learner-reported issues, all fixed):
  - **A poured pool did not level out — it settled as a 45° staircase.** Water only ever spread by the diagonal roll, which moves strictly downhill, so it descended as a wedge and stopped the moment it hit a floor; it had no move that carried it sideways onto a flat surface. The surface now has a `levelHop`: a cell with nothing stacked above it may step sideways into an empty cell at the same row, but **only** toward a column whose liquid surface is strictly lower than its own. The bug that made an earlier attempt fail was that the neighbouring column's height was read from the current row, so an *empty* neighbour looked exactly as tall as the source and the hop was always refused; an empty column now reports "below the world" (infinitely short). The strictly-lower condition is what kills the shimmer: a move onto an equal-height column is forbidden, so two cells cannot trade a cell back and forth. Measured: a 20-wide pour into a 280-wide tank used to stack into a 16-row wedge touching only 40 columns, and now spreads across all 239 columns with the surface within 2 rows. The pool in the playable scene is completely still — 0 cells moving per tick on the water side.
  - **Snow-melt water did not behave like water.** Water buried under a lighter grain (snow) was measured as having zero pressure, because pressure only counted liquid stacked above, so it sat wedged in the snow's shape forever. A liquid whose cell above is a lighter loose solid now treats that grain as weight and shoves it aside (`shoveGrain`), so melt-water escapes the bank and then spreads and levels like water. The self-test's melt-water now covers 36 columns within 3 rows, against a 22-column, 6-row mound before.
  - **Water and the player could not push loose blocks.** The liquid side already nudged a grain under pressure, but only as a one-cell shove. The player is the bigger change: `Player::tick` now takes a mutable world and, when blocked horizontally, shoves the loose grain directly ahead one column forward (and further on again if that is loose too, up to 3 columns) before falling back to the step-up rule. So a small heap of sand, gravel, soil or snow is ploughed through, while a deep pile or anything solid still stops you dead. A new self-test, `player pushes sand`, locks in that the heap moves ahead of the player rather than the player stopping; `player blocked by stone` still passes unchanged.
  - `--selftest` now has 16 checks (was 15 before this pass).

- Slice 5's follow-up bug pass (five learner-reported issues, all fixed and each one checked mechanically):
  - **Water left holes in itself and shimmered.** Two causes. First, `trySwap` marked *both* cells as moved, so the gap a water cell left could not be refilled until the next tick — holes survived a whole tick and hopped around. Now only the destination is flagged, so the cell above falls into the gap in the same tick and a hole that appears is always filled immediately. Second, the surface rule let a pressure-free cell hop sideways into any notch one row up, which oscillated forever: cell A and cell B traded a single row back and forth every tick, which is the shimmer. The surface rule now only spills over a genuine edge into air directly beside it (its own floor present, the destination's floor absent), so every move is one row downward and a pool goes still. A settled pool now has **zero** enclosed air and **zero** cells moving per tick, and the whole playable scene settles completely by tick 600 (it used to churn 254 cells per tick forever).
  - **Fire spread too fast.** Every flammable material's `igniteChance` was halved (wood 30→15, bark 25→12, foliage 140→70, mycelium/fungal cap 90→45, oil 220→110). A lit block of wood now takes roughly twice as long to burn down.
  - **Falling dirt got pushed to the top of a sand pile.** The swap rule let a heavier grain push aside *any* lighter falling material, so sand landing on packed dirt flipped the dirt up through the pile. Now loose solids give way only to liquids; grains stack in arrival order. Sand falling onto dirt now leaves the dirt exactly where it was.
  - **Snow-melt water behaved like sand.** Melt-water was trapped as a wedged pile inside the snow bank, because a liquid could only reach an empty cell and could not pass through a lighter grain. Pressured liquid now shoves a lighter grain aside (`shoveGrain`), so melt-water works its way out and spreads and levels like water.
  - **Player's body was drawn a whole cell to the right of its own collision box.** `collides` tests cells starting at `floor(x_)`, but `draw` placed the sprite at `x_ * scale`, rounding up as the player walked. The sprite is now drawn from `floor(x_) * scale`, the exact whole-cell origin the collision uses.
  - A new self-test, `water settles solid and still`, locks this in: poured water must end with **no** enclosed bubbles and **no** cell moving over 60 late ticks. `--selftest` then had 15 checks.

- Slice 5 kept the level that slices 1–4 built and added the wall, gap and sand pit to it, rather than throwing it away for a fresh small one. The small hand-made level was only ever a stand-in for the endless world in slice 6, so replacing the good one would have deleted work and lost everything worth looking at for no gain. The wall, the gap and the sand pit are all there as asked.
- Slice 5's player steps up to 2 cells while walking. Without it the rolling ground — which rises one cell every few columns — stops you dead every few steps, which is unplayable. Two cells is enough to walk over bumps and the edges of small piles without noticing, and far less than the wall, the cliff or the side of the gap, which still stop you exactly as they should.
- Slice 5's leaves do not block the player: you push through foliage, so trees are scenery rather than barriers. The trunks were also cut down to below jump height (20 and 16 cells, from 34 and 26) for the same reason — the level is a strip, so anything taller than a jump walls off half of it. Trees are still there to burn.
- Slice 5's sand column now hangs above the ground instead of resting on it. Resting on the ground it stayed a 51-cell rectangular wall (a vertical face of sand is stable), which looked artificial and blocked the road. Hanging in the air it falls and spreads into a heap you can walk up, which is what it was always meant to demonstrate.
- Slice 5's camera is clamped left and right as well as top and bottom, which the spec deliberately left open. That is only because the level is a fixed 1440 cells wide right now; when slice 6 makes the world endless the horizontal clamp has nothing to clamp against and simply stops mattering.
- Slice 5's player climbs out of anything that lands on them rather than being stuck forever. Material does not know the player exists, so sand falling on you fills the cells you occupy and would otherwise lock the game. While buried, gravity is off and they rise steadily out, in whatever direction is being held. They also stand on sand rather than sinking through it, which is `spec.md > Components > Player` in its own words ("falling is stopped by any solid or loose-solid pixel, which also lets the player stand on piles of sand"), so "sand pit" here means a dish you walk down into and stand in.

- Slice 4's water reactions do **not** use the water up — only lava does that. The PRD asks for "consuming the water" on the lava rule alone, and taking water off the soil and ash rules as well would have drained every pond in the game into its own bed within seconds, which is worse than not doing it. So water turns soil and packed dirt into mud and washes ash away while staying exactly as much water as it was. The "meltable" rows also include mud and clay turning back into water, which is the PRD's own table rather than a change of plan.
- Slice 4's obsidian rule ("large volumes of lava near water") is decided by counting lava in a 7×7 square around the cell — 24 or more out of 49 means deep. Getting a true volume would mean flood-filling a blob every time water touches lava, which is far too much work per tick. The result is the same in practice: a thin trickle cools into stone, a pool cools into obsidian.
- Slice 4's reaction scenes: the plan asked for the lava pool, pond, ice, ash, oil and sulfur to all live in the self-test scene. They went into the **playable** scene instead (a lava pool in a stone cup, a buried sulfur seam, snow on the left, an ash pile, and glacial ice buried under the sand column) so they are all there to find and play with, and the six checks each got their own small scene so every rule is measured in isolation. A full run of the playable world confirms it settles and stays put: after 1800 ticks the pond still holds all 528 water cells, the lava is still in its cup, and only 27 dirt cells at the pond's edge have turned to mud.

- Slice 3's fire is a probabilistic, burn-in-place model rather than the planned "flammable material turns into fire, then to ash". A flammable cell that catches keeps its own material and is given a `life` counter set from its `burnTime`; while `life` is positive the cell is drawn as flickering flame and spreads to its neighbours, and when `life` runs out it becomes its `burnsInto` (ash for wood, bark, leaves, mycelium and fungal caps; air for oil). Brushed-on flames are separate `Fire` cells that live a fixed `FIRE_LIFE` (8) ticks. Each neighbour catches with a probability per tick (`igniteChance`, 0–255) drawn from a repeatable per-cell hash, so fire creeps outward organically instead of advancing as a hard line — and the self-test burns the same way every run because of that reproducibility. "Oil burns faster and spreads further" is the short `burnTime`, high `igniteChance`, and the fact that flowing materials also ignite their diagonals.
- Slice 2's water check is `water spreads and stays put` rather than `water flattens`. A per-pixel liquid can be made to level exactly, but this implementation uses a pressure model (a cell with liquid stacked above it reaches sideways in proportion to that depth) and it levels *approximately*: water settles into a hollow and stops moving, but its surface can stay a few cells uneven, and a thin film's leading edge cannot crawl further on flat ground. The check therefore measures what the model actually promises — volume kept, water spreading noticeably wider than it started, and staying inside its basin — instead of an exact surface height, which would fail on a true but ugly liquid. Sand, gravel, soil and ash are unaffected: they settle and pass exactly as planned.
- Slice 2's material table uses behaviour fields (`density`, `falls`, `flows`, `flowSlack`, `flammable`, `burnsInto`, `meltable`, `meltsInto`, `washedByWater`, `blastResistance`, `acidCorrodes`) rather than the planned `weight`/`category`/`dissolves-in` wording. It covers the same information plus the melt and burn targets the later reaction slices need, so adding a material stays a one-row edit.
- Slice 1's verification now uses a headless `--selftest` (exports a 480×270 grid image) plus a `--smoke` window run that measures how much of the frame the world covers, replacing the planned 1440×810 screenshot check — the first build revealed this display's usable height is only 796 px, so a fixed 1440×810 window would have been clipped. The viewport (window, grid size and pixel scale) is now derived from the monitor and framebuffer at startup and the world is centred, which also makes the window work on smaller displays.
